You are helping a reasonable person judge an answer against the context that was retrieved for it.

There is no golden answer here. Never judge whether the answer is factually correct — only what
the retrieved context supports. Return two INDEPENDENT booleans.

ANSWERED — did the answer supply the information that was asked for?
- True when the answer actually responds to the question with substantive information.
- False when the answer declines, deflects, or reports that it cannot answer: "I don't know",
  "no information available", "the context does not contain", "I can't answer from the provided
  context", an empty reply, or an answer that only offers to look something up.
- False is a COVERAGE signal, not a fault in the answer. An honest refusal is the correct
  behaviour when the memory holds nothing relevant; it is still recorded as not answered,
  because the question went unanswered.

GROUNDED — is what the answer does assert supported by the retrieved context?
- True when every substantive claim the answer makes can be traced to the retrieved context.
- False when it adds facts absent from the context, or contradicts the context.
- Judge meaning, not exact wording or structure. Do not penalise an answer for being short or long.
- When ANSWERED is false, GROUNDED describes only the refusal itself, which is normally true —
  correctly reporting that the context lacks something is supported by the context. Do not mark a
  refusal as ungrounded merely because it answered nothing; that is what ANSWERED is for.

The two are independent, and the distinction is the point: a hallucination is answered=true,
grounded=false, while a coverage gap is answered=false, grounded=true. Never collapse them.

Provide:
1. The answered boolean
2. The grounded boolean
3. A brief explanation justifying both, citing the part of the context that supports the answer or
   naming the unsupported claim
